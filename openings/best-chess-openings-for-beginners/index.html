Opening guide

The best chess openings for beginners

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 15, 202611 min read

You don't need to know dozens of openings to play good chess. Most beginners only need three: one opening for White, one defense against 1.e4, and one against 1.d4. This guide shows the best choices, why they work, and how to pick the right one for your style.

The short answer: play the Italian Game or the London System with White, the Caro-Kann or Scandinavian against 1.e4, and the Queen's Gambit Declined against 1.d4.

First, learn the principles

Every good opening follows the same five rules. If you understand them, you'll find decent moves even when your opponent surprises you.

The best opening moves in chess

The best first moves for White are 1.e4 and 1.d4. Each one puts a pawn in the center and opens a line for a bishop, which is exactly what the five rules ask for. 1.c4 and 1.Nf3 are just as sound, but they lead to slower games that are harder to handle when you're new.

First moveWhat it doesOpenings it leads to
1.e4Takes the center and frees the queen and the light-squared bishopItalian Game, Ruy Lopez, Scotch, Vienna
1.d4Takes the center with a pawn the queen already protectsLondon System, Queen's Gambit
1.c4Fights for the d5 square from the sideEnglish Opening
1.Nf3Develops a knight and keeps every plan openUsually turns into a 1.d4 or 1.c4 opening

As Black, the best replies follow the same idea. Against 1.e4, play 1...e5 or 1...c6. Against 1.d4, play 1...d5. All three put a pawn in the center, or prepare to on the very next move.

Best openings for White

Italian Game: the best first opening

The Italian Game starts 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. In three moves you have a pawn in the center, two pieces out and a bishop aiming at f7, the weakest square in Black's camp. You can castle on move four or five. It follows every opening rule, so the habits you build here carry over to every other opening you learn later.

London System: the easiest to learn

In the London System you play the same setup against almost anything: pawns on d4, e3 and c3, a bishop on f4, knights on f3 and d2 and the other bishop on d3. There is very little to memorize and your king is always safe. The trade-off is that the games are quieter, so you'll see fewer tactics than with 1.e4.

Four Knights Game: pure development

The Four Knights Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nc3 Nf6) brings out all four knights before anything else happens. Nothing is sharp and nothing is forced, which makes it a good place to practice developing, castling and only then making a plan.

Queen's Gambit: the ambitious choice

The Queen's Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4) offers a side pawn to pull Black's d5 pawn away from the center. It isn't a real sacrifice, because Black can't hold on to the extra pawn for long. You learn a lot about central control, but there is more to study than in the London.

Once you're comfortable, try the Ruy Lopez for deeper strategy, the Scotch for open positions, or the Vienna as a surprise weapon.

Best defenses against 1.e4

Caro-Kann Defense: solid and simple

The Caro-Kann starts 1.e4 c6, preparing 2...d5 with a pawn ready to recapture. Your light-squared bishop usually gets out to f5 before you play ...e6, so you don't end up with a bishop stuck behind its own pawns. The pawn structure is healthy, and beginners rarely get mated quickly in it.

Scandinavian Defense: the least to remember

The Scandinavian strikes at once with 1.e4 d5. After 2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3 your queen has to move again, which costs a little time, but in return you play nearly the same setup every game: ...c6, ...Bf5, ...e6 and ...Nf6.

Petrov Defense: for players who like 1...e5

The Petrov (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nf6) answers an attack on your pawn with an attack on White's. It is very solid once you know one trap: after 3.Nxe5, don't take on e4 straight away, because 4.Qe2 wins material. Play 3...d6 first to chase the knight, then take the pawn.

Playing 1...e5 and meeting the Italian Game from the Black side is also a great choice, because you learn both sides of the same positions. If you want sharp, fighting games, the Sicilian is the most popular choice at every level, and the French suits patient counterattackers.

Best defenses against 1.d4

Queen's Gambit Declined: the classical answer

In the Queen's Gambit Declined (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6) you keep a strong pawn on d5 and develop behind it with ...Nf6, ...Be7 and castling. World champions have trusted it for over a century, and the plans are easy to understand.

Slav Defense: solid, with a freer bishop

The Slav (1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6) supports d5 with the c-pawn instead of the e-pawn. That keeps the diagonal open for your light-squared bishop, which is the piece that often gets shut in after 2...e6.

Attacking players can try the King's Indian; strategic players will enjoy the Nimzo-Indian.

Best chess openings for intermediate players

When the basics feel easy and you rarely lose pieces for nothing, usually somewhere around a 1200 to 1600 online rating, you can pick openings with more ideas in them:

  • Ruy Lopez: the natural step up from the Italian Game, with long-term pressure instead of quick threats.
  • Scotch Game: opens the center on move three for clear, active piece play.
  • Sicilian Defense: the fighting answer to 1.e4. Unbalanced positions where Black plays to win.
  • French Defense: a solid pawn chain and a clear plan of attacking White's center.
  • King's Indian Defense: Black lets White build a big center, then attacks it and the king behind it.
  • Nimzo-Indian Defense: a strategic defense to 1.d4 built on pinning the knight on c3.
  • English Opening: 1.c4, a flexible way to control the center from the side.
  • Queen's Gambit Accepted, the Pirc and the Dutch: three more defenses for Black once the basics feel easy.

How to choose your opening

  • If you like attacking, play the Italian Game or the Scotch with White, and later the Sicilian or the King's Indian with Black.
  • If you like safe, steady positions, play the London System, the Caro-Kann and the Queen's Gambit Declined.
  • If you have very little time to study, the London System and the Scandinavian need the least memorizing.
  • If you can't decide, take the 1-minute opening quiz and it will pick for you.

How to learn an opening without memorizing

  1. Learn the first five or six moves and say out loud why each one is played.
  2. Learn the plan, not just the moves: where your pieces go, which side you castle on and which pawn you push next.
  3. Play at least 20 games with it before you judge it. Every lesson on CastleEarly has a practice mode against the computer.
  4. Check your games afterwards. The free analysis shows the first move where you left the opening and what was better.
  5. Learn one trap in your opening, so you can spot it from both sides.

What beginners should avoid

  • Bringing the queen out early to chase quick checkmates like Scholar's Mate. It works against total beginners, then stops working fast.
  • Relying on trappy gambits such as the Englund Gambit as your main opening. Sound gambits like the King's Gambit, Evans Gambit and Danish Gambit are great for learning to attack, once you know the basics. They are fun in fast games, but you're simply worse when your opponent knows the answer.
  • Memorizing long lines without understanding them. The first 6–8 moves and the plans are enough.
  • Switching openings every week. Stick with one choice for a few months so you learn its typical positions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best chess opening for beginners?

For White, the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) is the best first opening, because it follows every opening rule and leads to open, instructive games. If you want the least to memorize, play the London System instead.

What is the best first move in chess?

1.e4 and 1.d4 are the two best first moves. Both take the center and open a line for a bishop. 1.e4 leads to more open, tactical games, which is why most coaches recommend it for beginners.

What is the easiest chess opening to learn?

The London System for White and the Scandinavian Defense for Black. In both you play nearly the same setup every game, whatever your opponent does.

How many openings should a beginner learn?

Three is enough: one for White, one against 1.e4 and one against 1.d4. Add more only when you're comfortable with those.

Should beginners play 1.e4 or 1.d4?

Both are fine. 1.e4 usually leads to open, tactical games, which are great for learning. 1.d4 leads to more strategic positions. The London System is a very easy way to start with 1.d4. If you prefer a quieter start with a side pawn, try the English Opening (1.c4).

What are the most common chess openings?

In beginner and club games you'll meet the Italian Game, the Ruy Lopez, the Sicilian Defense, the French Defense, the Caro-Kann, the Queen's Gambit and the London System most often. Knowing the first few moves of each is enough to recognize them.

Is the Sicilian Defense good for beginners?

It is a sound opening, but it has a lot of theory and the positions are sharp. Most beginners do better starting with the Caro-Kann or 1...e5 and adding the Sicilian later.

Do I need to memorize chess openings?

No. Below club level, understanding the ideas matters far more than memorizing. Know the first five or six moves of your opening, follow the five opening rules after that, and you'll get a good position in most games.

What's the best chess opening of all?

There isn't one. All the main openings are sound. The best opening for you is the one whose positions you understand and enjoy.
