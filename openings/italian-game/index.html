Opening for White · 1.e4

Italian Game: a complete beginner's guide

By HussainUpdated October 28, 20268 min read

The Italian Game is one of the oldest chess openings and still one of the best for beginners. White develops quickly, castles early, and points a bishop straight at Black's weakest square. Below you'll find the main line on a board you can step through, the plans for both sides, the traps to watch for, and a Practice mode to test yourself.

Quick answer

The Italian Game starts 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. White develops the bishop to c4, where it aims at Black's weakest square, f7, then castles and builds the center with c3 and d4. It's one of the best openings for beginners because the ideas are simple and every move develops a piece.

The main line, move by move

This is the modern, calm version of the Italian (often called the Giuoco Pianissimo, "very quiet game"). It's the setup most club players and many grandmasters use today. Press ▶ to step through it, then switch to Practice and play White's moves yourself.

Why the Italian Game works

Every move in the main line follows the basic opening rules: a pawn in the center, knights before bishops, and castling within the first ten moves. That's why it's such a good opening to learn first. Even when you forget the exact moves, you can usually find a sensible one.

The bishop on c4 is the heart of the opening. On move three it already looks at f7. At the start of the game, that pawn is protected only by the king. Many quick wins in the Italian come from attacks on that square, so Black has to be careful from the very beginning.

White's plan after castling

  • Re1 to support the e4 pawn and get ready for the center to open.
  • Nbd2–f1–g3: a famous knight route that brings the queenside knight over to help on the kingside.
  • a4 to stop Black from chasing the bishop with ...b5. If the bishop is attacked, Bb3 or Ba2 keeps it on the same diagonal.
  • d4 at the right moment, to take over the center once everything is protected.

Black's plan

  • Finish development and castle, exactly like White.
  • Play ...a6 and ...Ba7 so the bishop can't be traded off by Na4.
  • Play ...h6 to stop White's bishop or knight from coming to g5.
  • Look for the ...d5 break to free the position when White isn't ready for it.

What if Black plays something else on move 3?

The main line above starts with 3...Bc5. You'll also meet these replies:

3...Nf6 — the Two Knights Defense

Black attacks e4 straight away instead of developing the bishop. The simplest answer for beginners is 4.d3, protecting the pawn and keeping the same calm structure as the main line. If you'd rather attack, 4.Ng5 leads to the famous Fried Liver Attack. Here's the calm way first:

Watch out for the Fried Liver if you play Black. After 3...Nf6, White can play the aggressive 4.Ng5, attacking f7 twice. After 4...d5 5.exd5, the natural 5...Nxd5? lets White sacrifice with 6.Nxf7! and drag Black's king into the open. The safe reply for Black is 5...Na5, chasing the bishop away.

3...Be7 — the Hungarian Defense

A very solid, passive choice. Black avoids every sharp line and just prepares to castle. White should keep developing with d3 or d4, castle, and enjoy a slightly freer position.

A trap every Italian player must know

After 3.Bc4, some opponents play 3...Nd4, which looks like a mistake because it leaves the e5 pawn hanging. It's a trap called the Blackburne Shilling Gambit. If White grabs the pawn with 4.Nxe5? and then goes after f7, Black wins in a few moves. Step through it to see how:

How White avoids it: after 3...Nd4, don't take on e5. Play 4.Nxd4 exd4 and then castle, or simply 4.O-O or 4.c3. White is better because Black moved the same knight twice for no real reason.

Common beginner mistakes in the Italian

  • Attacking too early. Moves like Ng5 or Qh5 without enough support usually waste time against a careful opponent.
  • Forgetting to castle. The center can open suddenly, and an uncastled king gets caught.
  • Letting the bishop get traded. When Black plays ...Na5, retreat the bishop to b3 (or prepare a4 earlier) so you keep your best piece.
  • Pinning with Bg5 too soon. Black can often answer ...h6 and ...g5, gaining space with tempo.
  • Pushing d4 before it's protected. Prepare it with c3 first, as in the main line.

A little history

The Italian Game was analyzed by Italian players as early as the 1500s and 1600s, which is how it got its name. "Giuoco Piano" means "quiet game" in Italian. For a long time it was considered old-fashioned, but in recent decades top players brought the slow c3 and d3 setup back into fashion, and today it's played at every level, from beginners to the world's elite.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Italian Game good for beginners?

Yes. It follows the basic opening principles, the plans are easy to understand, and it teaches you to develop fast and castle early. It's one of the most recommended first openings for White.

What is the difference between the Italian Game and the Giuoco Piano?

The Italian Game is the position after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. The Giuoco Piano is one branch of it, reached when Black answers 3...Bc5.

What should White play against the Two Knights Defense?

For beginners, 4.d3 is the simplest. It protects the e4 pawn and leads to the same kind of calm position as the main line. The sharper 4.Ng5 is fine too, but it requires much more memorization; see our Fried Liver Attack guide.

How many moves of the Italian do I need to know?

The first six to eight moves of the main line are enough for most club games. Understanding the plans matters more than memorizing deeper moves.
