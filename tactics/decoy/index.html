The decoy: lure a piece onto a fatal square

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 16, 20263 min read

Sometimes a tactic would work perfectly if an enemy piece stood one square over. A decoy makes that happen: you offer something that has to be taken, and the piece that takes it lands exactly where you want it.

Quick answer

A decoy is a tactic, usually a sacrifice, that lures an enemy piece onto a particular square where it can be forked, pinned, skewered or checkmated. The king is the most common victim, because a checking sacrifice often has to be captured.

Also called
Attraction, luring sacrifice

A sacrifice with check
Level

What is a decoy in chess?

A decoy starts from the end. You imagine a fork, a skewer or a mate that needs an enemy piece on one specific square. Then you look for a way to force it there, normally by sacrificing something on that square with check.

The sacrifice doesn't have to be cheap. Giving up a rook to win a queen is a good trade, and giving up a queen to force checkmate is an even better one.

How to spot a decoy

  • Look for a tactic that almost works. If a knight fork would win the queen with the king one square to the side, ask how to move the king there.
  • Check every sacrifice that gives check. The king often has no choice but to capture.
  • Make sure the reply is forced. If the piece can decline the offer, the decoy fails.

How to defend against decoys

  • Before you capture a free piece, ask why it was offered. Look at the square you would land on and what could attack you there.
  • Decline when you can. If a move other than capturing gets you out of trouble, consider it.
  • Keep your queen and king off squares a knight can fork after one forced move.

Frequently asked questions

What is a decoy in chess?

A decoy is a move, usually a sacrifice, that lures an enemy piece onto a square where it will be hit by a tactic such as a fork or a checkmate.

What is the difference between a decoy and a deflection?

A decoy draws a piece to a bad square. A deflection drives a piece away from a good square it needs to stay on.

Is a decoy always a sacrifice?

Almost always. The piece is lured by something it has to capture, so you usually give up material and win back more.
