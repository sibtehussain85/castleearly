The desperado: a lost piece takes something with it

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 16, 20263 min read

When one of your pieces is going to be captured whatever you do, it has nothing left to lose. Use it to grab a pawn or a piece on its way out. That piece is called a desperado.

Quick answer

A desperado is a piece that is trapped or certain to be captured, and so gives itself up for as much material as it can get. The term is also used for a piece that sacrifices itself to bring about stalemate or perpetual check.

Typical case
A trapped piece, or both sides with a piece attacked
Goal
Get a pawn or a piece for something you would lose anyway
Level
Intermediate
Related
Trapped piece, in-between move

What is a desperado in chess?

The reasoning is simple arithmetic. If your knight is going to be lost for nothing, then losing it for a pawn is a pawn better, and losing it for a bishop is a fair trade.

Desperados often appear when both players have a piece attacked at the same time. Whoever moves first can use the doomed piece to capture something, and the opponent may then answer with a desperado of their own. In these positions it pays to calculate every capture before choosing one.

How to spot a desperado

  • Ask whether the piece can be saved. If it truly can't, list everything it is able to capture.
  • Choose the most valuable capture, and prefer one that comes with check.
  • When pieces on both sides are hanging, look for captures before you recapture.

How to defend against a desperado

  • When you trap a piece, check what it can capture before it dies. If needed, protect those targets first; the trapped piece isn't going anywhere.
  • Take the trapped piece in a way that keeps your pawns and pieces protected.

Frequently asked questions

What is a desperado in chess?

A desperado is a piece that is going to be lost anyway and captures as much as it can before it is taken.

Why is it called a desperado?

The word means a reckless outlaw with nothing to lose. A doomed chess piece is in the same situation, so it can afford to do anything.

Can a desperado be used to draw?

Yes. A piece can keep offering itself to force stalemate or perpetual check. A rook that does this is often called a rampant rook or crazy rook.
