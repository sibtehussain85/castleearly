King and rook vs king: the checkmate step by step

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 16, 20264 min read

King and rook against king is the second basic checkmate, and it is a little harder than the one with the queen. The rook can't trap the king alone, so your king has to do half the work. Once you understand the one key position, the whole thing becomes mechanical.

Quick answer

To checkmate with a king and rook against a king: use the rook to cut the enemy king off along a rank or file, bring your king to face the enemy king with one square between them, then check with the rook to drive the king back one row. Repeat until it is on the edge, where the same check is mate. If the kings aren't facing each other, make a waiting move with the rook. It takes at most 16 moves.

The method step by step

  1. Cut the king off. Put your rook on a rank or file between the enemy king and the middle of the board. The king can never cross that line.
  2. Bring your king. Walk it toward the enemy king. Your aim is to get the two kings facing each other with exactly one square between them. This is called the opposition.
  3. Check. When the kings face each other and it is your move, give check with the rook along the line the enemy king stands on. Your king covers the three squares in front, so the enemy king has to step back one row. The cage is now one row smaller.
  4. Repeat. Bring your king forward again, get the kings facing each other, and check. When the enemy king is on the edge, the check is checkmate.

Practice it here. You play White, and the computer defends as well as it can.

The waiting move

Often you reach a position where the kings are almost facing each other, but it is your turn and any king move would let the enemy king slip sideways. The answer is a waiting move: slide the rook one square along the line it is already guarding. Nothing changes, except that now your opponent has to move, and the only moves available walk into the position you wanted.

Two more tips:

  • If the enemy king attacks your rook, move the rook far away along the same rank or file. It keeps guarding the line from the other side of the board.
  • Keep your king a knight's move from the enemy king when you approach. From there, any sideways step by the defender runs into the opposition.

The mating position

The final position always looks the same: the enemy king is on the edge, your king stands directly opposite it with one square between them, and the rook gives check along the edge. In a corner, your king can stand one file over.

Common mistakes

  • Checking too early. A rook check when the kings are not facing each other just lets the king walk forward. Wait for the right position.
  • Letting the king attack your rook. Keep the rook at a distance, and move it away along its line if the king comes close.
  • Trying to mate in the middle of the board. It can't be done. Rook mates only happen on the edge.
  • Running out of moves. The 50-move rule applies. With the right method you need 16 moves at most, so there is plenty of time, as long as you don't go in circles.

Frequently asked questions

Can you checkmate with a king and rook?

Yes. King and rook against a lone king is always a win with correct play.

How many moves does a king and rook checkmate take?

At most 16 moves from any position with perfect play.

Can a rook checkmate without the king?

One rook can't. It needs the king's help. Two rooks can checkmate without the king, using the ladder mate.

What is a waiting move?

A move that changes nothing important and passes the turn to your opponent. In this endgame you slide the rook one square along the line it guards.

Is king and rook against king harder than king and queen?

A little. The rook covers fewer squares, so your king has to help push the enemy king back, and it takes more moves.
