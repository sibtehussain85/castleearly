King and queen vs king: the checkmate step by step

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 16, 20264 min read

You win a queen, trade everything else off, and now you have to finish the job with just a king and queen. This is the most important checkmate in chess, because so many games come down to it. The method is simple, always works, and takes about a minute to learn.

Quick answer

To checkmate with a king and queen against a king: use the queen to push the enemy king to the edge by staying a knight's move away from it, stop when it can only shuffle along the edge, bring your own king next to it, then give checkmate with the queen protected by your king. From any position it takes at most ten moves, and the one thing to avoid is stalemate.

The method in four steps

  1. Trap the king in a box. Place your queen a knight's move away from the enemy king. From there she fences it into a rectangle and can't be attacked.
  2. Follow the king. Whatever move the king makes, make the same move with your queen: if it goes one square left, she goes one square left. She stays a knight's move away and the box shrinks without any thought.
  3. Stop when the king reaches the edge. As soon as the king is on the last rank or file and has only two squares to move between, leave the queen where she is. Moving her closer is how stalemates happen.
  4. Bring your king, then mate. Walk your king over until it stands near the enemy king. Then give check with the queen on a square your king protects, right in front of the enemy king, or along the edge.

Practice it here. You play White, and the computer defends as well as it can.

How to avoid stalemate

A queen covers so many squares that she can take all of them away from a cornered king. If the king is not in check and has no move, the game is a stalemate and you have thrown away the win.

  • The danger position is a queen a knight's move from a king in the corner. With the black king on h8, a white queen on f7 or g6 leaves it with no squares.
  • Before every queen move, count the king's squares. If the answer is zero and you are not giving check, pick another move.
  • When the king is on the edge, move your king, not your queen. The queen has already done her job.

The mating positions

Every king and queen checkmate ends in one of two ways. Either the queen stands directly in front of the enemy king with your king protecting her, or she checks along the edge while your king covers the squares in front. Try the first one.

Common mistakes

  • Giving check after check. Random checks let the king run back to the middle. Shrink the box first.
  • Leaving the queen where the king can attack her. A knight's move away, she is always safe.
  • Forgetting your own king. The queen can't give mate alone. You need the king's help for the last step.
  • Rushing at the end. Most stalemates happen in the last three moves.

Frequently asked questions

Can you checkmate with just a king and queen?

Yes. King and queen against a lone king is always a win, and it is the most common basic checkmate.

How many moves does a king and queen checkmate take?

At most ten moves from any position with perfect play. With the simple box method it may take a few more, which is fine.

Can a queen checkmate without the king?

Not against a lone king. The queen needs her own king to protect her or to cover escape squares for the final move.

How do you avoid stalemate with a queen?

Once the enemy king is on the edge, stop moving the queen and bring your king. Before any queen move, check that the enemy king still has a legal move.

What is the box method in chess?

A way of forcing a king to the edge by using the queen or rook to fence it into a rectangle and making that rectangle smaller move by move.
