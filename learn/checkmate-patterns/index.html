Checkmate patterns: 10 mates every player should know

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 16, 20264 min read

Strong players don't work out every checkmate from scratch. They recognize it. Certain arrangements of pieces around a king come up again and again, and once you know them you start to see mates two and three moves before they happen. Here are ten patterns worth learning first, each with a mate in one to solve.

Quick answer

A checkmate pattern is a recurring arrangement of pieces that traps the king. The most important ones for beginners are the back-rank mate, the ladder mate with two rooks, the queen-and-king mate, the smothered mate and the queen-and-bishop battery mate on h7. Named patterns such as the Arabian, Anastasia's, Boden's, epaulette and opera mates come up regularly in real games.

1. Back-rank mate

A rook or queen gives check on the last rank, and the king is trapped behind its own pawns. This is the most common checkmate in beginner games. More on the back-rank mate →

2. Ladder mate

Two rooks (or a rook and a queen) work together. One guards a rank so the king can't step forward, and the other checks on the edge. More on the ladder mate →

3. Queen and king mate

The queen gives check right next to the enemy king, protected by her own king, so she can't be captured. It is sometimes called the kiss of death. This is how every king and queen endgame finishes. Learn the full method →

4. Queen and bishop on h7

A queen lands on h7 (or h2) next to the castled king, with a bishop behind her on the same diagonal. The king can't take the queen because the bishop protects her. More on batteries →

5. Smothered mate

A knight gives check to a king that is completely boxed in by its own pieces. Nothing can block a knight's check, so if the knight can't be captured, it is mate. Full lesson on the smothered mate →

6. Arabian mate

A rook and a knight trap the king in the corner. The knight protects the rook and also covers the king's last escape square. It is one of the oldest recorded checkmates, known from Arabic chess manuscripts written more than a thousand years ago.

7. Anastasia's mate

A knight and a rook mate a king on the edge of the board. The knight takes away the two squares next to the king, the king's own pawn blocks a third, and the rook checks along the file. The name comes from a novel published in 1803, Anastasia and the Game of Chess.

8. Boden's mate

Two bishops on crossing diagonals mate a king that is hemmed in by its own pieces, usually after it has castled queenside. It is named after the English master Samuel Boden, who played it in 1853.

9. Epaulette mate

The king has one of its own pieces on each side, like the epaulettes on the shoulders of a uniform. Those pieces block its escape to the sides, and a queen two squares in front covers everything else.

10. Opera mate

A rook checks on the back rank, protected by a bishop, against a king that is blocked by its own pieces. It is named after the Opera Game, which Paul Morphy won in a box at the Paris opera in 1858.

Checkmates in the opening

Some mates happen in the first few moves, and every beginner should know them so as not to fall for them:

  • Scholar's Mate: the four-move checkmate on f7 with queen and bishop.
  • Fool's Mate: the fastest possible checkmate, in two moves.
  • The Englund Gambit trap and the Siberian Trap: queen mates that punish one careless developing move.

How to learn checkmate patterns

  • Learn the shape. Each pattern works on any edge of the board and for either color. Remember which squares each piece covers.
  • Solve mate-in-one puzzles daily. Speed matters more than difficulty at first. The tactics page has more to practice on.
  • Check for the pattern in your own games. After each game, run the free analysis and look for mates you missed.
  • Then work backwards. Once you know the final position, you will start to find the sacrifices that lead to it.

Frequently asked questions

What is a checkmate pattern?

A checkmate pattern is a typical arrangement of pieces that checkmates a king and comes up again and again in different games.

What is the most common checkmate?

The back-rank mate. A rook or queen checks on the last rank and the king is trapped behind its own pawns.

How many checkmate patterns are there?

There is no fixed number. Around 30 to 40 have traditional names, and about ten of them cover most of what happens in club games.

What is the fastest checkmate in chess?

Fool's Mate, which takes two moves: 1.f3 e5 2.g4 Qh4#.

What is the 4-move checkmate called?

Scholar's Mate: 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6 4.Qxf7#.

How do I get better at spotting checkmates?

Learn the basic patterns, then solve many easy mate-in-one and mate-in-two puzzles so that you recognize the shapes quickly.
