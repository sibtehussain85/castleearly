Special moves: castling, en passant and promotion

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 17, 20264 min read

Three moves in chess don't follow the normal rules for each piece. They confuse a lot of beginners, but each one is simple once you've tried it on a board.

Almost every chess move follows the simple patterns of the six pieces. Three moves break those patterns: castling, en passant and promotion. Beginners often don't know them, and some players even think en passant is a joke, so here is exactly how each one works, with a board to try it on.

Quick answer

Chess has three special moves. Castling moves the king two squares toward a rook, and the rook jumps to the square on the king's other side. En passant lets a pawn capture an enemy pawn that has just moved two squares past it. Promotion turns a pawn that reaches the last row into a queen, rook, bishop or knight.

Castling

Castling is the only move where two pieces move at once. The king moves two squares toward a rook, and the rook jumps over to the other side of the king. It keeps your king safe and brings the rook into play.

You can castle only if:

  • neither the king nor that rook has moved yet;
  • all squares between them are empty;
  • the king is not in check, doesn't pass through an attacked square, and doesn't land in check.

Short castling (kingside) is written O-O; long castling (queenside) is O-O-O. Full guide to castling →

En passant

En passant (French for "in passing") is a special pawn capture. If an enemy pawn moves two squares forward and lands right beside your pawn, you can capture it as if it had moved only one square.

You must do it immediately, on the very next move, or the chance is gone. Full guide to en passant →

On this board, Black has just played the pawn from d7 to d5. Your e5 pawn can take it by moving to d6.

Promotion

When a pawn reaches the last rank, it promotes: it turns into a queen, rook, bishop or knight of your choice. Almost everyone chooses a queen, but a knight or rook is sometimes better (see underpromotion).

You can have more than one queen this way. Full guide to pawn promotion →

Why chess has special moves

All three were added to make the game faster and fairer as the rules developed in Europe. When pawns were given the right to move two squares on their first move, en passant was added so that a pawn couldn't use that double step to slip past an enemy pawn without risk. Castling took its modern form in the 16th and 17th centuries, after the queen and bishop became long-range pieces and kings needed a quicker route to safety. Promotion is older still: a pawn that reaches the far side has turned into a stronger piece since medieval times.

Common mistakes with special moves

  • Castling out of check or through an attacked square. Both are illegal.
  • Waiting a move to capture en passant. The chance disappears after one move.
  • Promoting on autopilot. A queen is almost always right, but check that it doesn't cause stalemate.
  • Touching the rook first when castling. Over the board, touch the king first; castling is a king move.

Frequently asked questions

Can you castle if the rook is attacked?

Yes. Only the king's squares matter: the king can't be in check, pass through an attacked square or land on one. The rook may be attacked.

Is en passant mandatory?

No. It's optional, like any capture, unless it's the only legal move. But if you don't do it right away, you lose the right to do it.

Can a pawn promote to a king?

No. A pawn can become a queen, rook, bishop or knight, but never a king.

How many special moves are there in chess?

Three: castling, en passant and pawn promotion.

Can any piece capture en passant?

No. Only a pawn can capture en passant, and only an enemy pawn that has just moved two squares to land beside it.

Can you castle after the king has moved?

No. Once the king has moved, even if it returns to its starting square, castling is no longer allowed for the rest of the game.

Can you have two queens in chess?

Yes. Promoting a pawn gives you a new queen even if your first queen is still on the board.
