How chess pieces move

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 17, 20266 min read

Each chess piece moves in its own way. On this page, every piece has its own board: tap it and dots show every square it can reach. (Each board has two kings too, because every chess position needs them.)

Each chess piece has its own way of moving, and every rule in chess builds on these six patterns. Below you can try each piece on its own board, then see how they capture, how much they are worth and where each one is strongest.

Quick answer

The king moves one square in any direction. The queen moves any number of squares in any direction, the rook in straight lines and the bishop diagonally. The knight jumps in an L-shape and is the only piece that can leap over others. Pawns move forward one square (two on their first move) and capture diagonally.

The king

The king moves one square in any direction: forward, backward, sideways or diagonally. It's the most important piece, but not the strongest. It can never move into check.

Value: priceless. If it's checkmated, the game is over.

The queen

The queen is the most powerful piece. She moves any number of squares in a straight line or along a diagonal, as long as nothing is in the way.

Value: about 9 points.

The rook

The rook moves any number of squares along a rank (row) or file (column). Rooks are strongest on open files and on the seventh rank.

Value: about 5 points.

The bishop

The bishop moves any number of squares diagonally. Each bishop stays on one color for the whole game: you have one light-squared and one dark-squared bishop.

Value: about 3 points.

The knight

The knight moves in an L-shape: two squares in one direction, then one square to the side. It's the only piece that can jump over other pieces, which makes its forks so dangerous.

Value: about 3 points.

The pawn

The pawn moves straight forward one square, or two squares on its very first move. It captures diagonally, one square forward. Here the white pawn can move to d3 or d4, or capture the black pawn on c3.

Value: 1 point, but a pawn that reaches the last rank becomes a queen.

Pieces near the middle of the board reach more squares than pieces on the edge. A knight in the corner reaches only two squares, which is why the old saying goes "a knight on the rim is dim."

How the pieces capture

  • Every piece except the pawn captures the same way it moves. It lands on the enemy piece's square, and the captured piece is removed from the board.
  • Pawns capture differently. They move straight ahead but capture one square diagonally forward. A pawn can't capture the piece directly in front of it, and it can't move forward if that square is occupied.
  • You can't capture your own pieces, and only the knight can jump over pieces in its way.
  • The king can capture too, but only a piece that is not defended. Taking a defended piece would put the king in check, which is never allowed.
  • The king itself is never captured. When it is attacked and can't escape, that is checkmate and the game ends.

For example, giving a rook (5) for a knight (3) usually loses two points of material. This is called losing "the exchange."

Many players value a bishop slightly above a knight, and two bishops together (the "bishop pair") are usually worth a little more than a bishop and a knight. The values also change with the position, so treat them as a starting point.

Where each piece is strongest

  • Knights like closed positions and secure squares near the center, called outposts, where enemy pawns can't chase them away.
  • Bishops like open positions with long diagonals and few pawns in the way.
  • Rooks belong on open files, with no pawns on them, and on the seventh rank, where they attack the enemy pawns.
  • The queen is strongest when the board is open, but bringing her out too early lets your opponent chase her and gain time.
  • The king should stay safe, usually after castling, while there are many pieces on the board. In the endgame it becomes an attacking piece and should walk to the center.
  • Pawns are the soul of the position. Advanced pawns gain space; a pawn that reaches the last rank promotes.

Check, checkmate and stalemate

When a piece attacks the enemy king, that is check, and the king must get out of it on the very next move. If there is no way out, it is checkmate and the game is over. If a player is not in check but has no legal move at all, it is stalemate, which is a draw. See 10 checkmate patterns to learn the most common ways to finish a game.

Frequently asked questions

Which chess piece is the most powerful?

The queen. She combines the moves of the rook and the bishop.

Can any piece jump over others?

Only the knight. Every other piece is blocked by pieces in its way.

Can a pawn move backward?

No. Pawns only move forward, which is why every pawn move should be made carefully.

How does the knight move in chess?

In an L-shape: two squares forward, backward or sideways, then one square to the side. It is the only piece that can jump over other pieces.

Can the king capture?

Yes. The king can capture any enemy piece next to it, as long as that piece is not defended. Capturing a defended piece would put the king in check.

Can a pawn capture sideways or backward?

No. A pawn only captures one square diagonally forward. The one exception is en passant, which is also a diagonal forward capture.

How far can the queen move?

Any number of squares in a straight line or along a diagonal, until another piece blocks her. From a central square on an empty board she reaches 27 squares.

Can the king move two squares?

Only when castling. Otherwise the king always moves one square at a time.

Is a bishop or a knight better?

They are both worth about 3 points. Bishops are usually better in open positions and knights in closed ones.
