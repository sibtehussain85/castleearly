Stalemate in chess: what it is and how to avoid it

By CastleEarlyUpdated November 15, 20269 min read

Stalemate is the chess rule that turns a lot of winning positions into draws. It happens when the player whose turn it is has no legal move, but their king is not in check. Beginners throw away half points to it all the time, usually with an extra queen on the board. Here's how it works and how to make sure it never happens to you.

Quick answer

Stalemate is a draw. It happens when the player to move has no legal moves and their king is not in check. For example, with Black's king on a8, leaves Black with no move at all, so the game is drawn, while would have been checkmate. Each player gets half a point.

The stalemate rule: draw by stalemate

The official rule is short. Under Article 5.2.1 of the FIDE Laws of Chess, the game is drawn when the player to move has no legal move and their king is not in check. Three things have to be true at the same moment:

  1. It is your turn. Stalemate is only ever judged for the player who has to move.
  2. Your king is not in check. If it were, having no legal move would be checkmate.
  3. You have no legal move with any piece, not just the king. One pawn that can still step forward means the game goes on.

The game ends at once. Nobody has to claim the draw, and sites such as Chess.com and Lichess stop the game automatically and show a draw by stalemate. Each player scores half a point, exactly as in any other draw, however many pieces the stronger side has left.

What causes a stalemate?

Nearly every stalemate comes from one of these five situations:

  • A queen boxes in a lone king. The queen covers so many squares that one careless move takes away the king's last one. This is by far the most common cause in beginner games.
  • A rook pawn on the edge. With an a-pawn or an h-pawn, the defending king sits in the corner in front of it and simply runs out of squares.
  • Blocked pawns. If your opponent's only other pieces are pawns that are jammed against yours, those pawns have no moves, so everything depends on the king.
  • Pinned pieces. A piece that is pinned to its own king can't legally move. A player can have a knight or bishop on the board and still be stalemated.
  • A deliberate sacrifice. The losing side gives away its last piece that can move, often with check, so that the king is left with nothing to do.

How to avoid stalemate

  • Before every move, ask: will my opponent still have a legal move? If the answer is no and you're not giving check, choose another move.
  • Watch the knight's-jump pattern. A queen a knight's jump away from a cornered king, like , is the classic stalemate. is the mate.
  • Bring your king closer first. A queen can't give mate on her own. Leave the enemy king some room while your own king walks over to help.
  • Watch out for blocked pawns. If your opponent's only other pieces are blocked pawns, they can't move either.

Using stalemate to save a lost game

If you're losing badly, stalemate is your friend. Head for the corner with your king, and look for ways to give away your remaining pieces so that your king has no moves left. Strong players set stalemate traps in lost positions all the time, and beginners with a big material lead often fall for them.

Stalemate vs the other ways to draw

Stalemate is one of five ways a chess game can end in a draw. People often mix it up with the move-count rules, so here they are side by side:

Each of these is explained with examples in the five ways a chess game can end in a draw. There is no number of moves after which a game becomes stalemate. If you have heard "it's a stalemate after 50 moves", that is the 50-move rule, which is a different kind of draw.

Why is stalemate a draw and not a win?

In chess you win by checkmate, not by capturing the king, and a king is never allowed to step onto an attacked square. A stalemated player isn't in check and has no legal move, so the rules can't make them move and can't call it mate either. The only fair result left is a draw.

It wasn't always this way. In earlier centuries the result of stalemate changed from country to country, and in some places it even counted as a win for the player who was stalemated. The draw became the standard rule during the 1800s, and it gives the defending side something to play for right to the end.

Common mistakes

  • Grabbing every last pawn. Taking your opponent's last piece that can move may leave them with no moves at all.
  • Chasing the king with the queen alone. You need your king's help to give mate, so bring it closer.
  • Moving too fast when winning. Most stalemates happen in completely won positions, often in time trouble.

💡 Club-level tip

When you're winning with a queen, slow down at the end. Before each move, count the enemy king's free squares. If the answer would be zero and you're not giving check, stop and pick a different move.

Frequently asked questions

Is stalemate a win or a draw?

A draw. Each player gets half a point, no matter how much material is left on the board.

What is the difference between stalemate and checkmate?

In both, the side to move has no legal move. In checkmate the king is in check, so that player loses. In stalemate the king is not in check, so the game is a draw.

Can it be stalemate with other pieces still on the board?

Yes. If every one of a player's pieces is blocked or pinned and the king has no safe square, it's stalemate, even with plenty of material left.

What if the king has no moves but another piece can move?

Then it is not stalemate and the game goes on. Stalemate only happens when the player has no legal move at all.

Who wins in a stalemate?

Nobody. Stalemate is a draw, so each player gets half a point, even if one side has a queen and the other has only a king.

What causes a stalemate in chess?

The player to move has no legal move and is not in check. It usually happens when a queen takes away a lone king's last square, or when the defender's remaining pawns are blocked.

After how many moves is it a stalemate?

There is no move count. Stalemate depends only on the position. The 50-move rule and threefold repetition are separate ways to draw.

Is stalemate a draw on Chess.com and Lichess?

Yes. Both sites follow the standard rules and end the game automatically as a draw by stalemate.

Is stalemate a draw under official rules?

Yes. Under the official FIDE rules, and US Chess rules, stalemate ends the game in a draw.
